Making people want to unbox a credit card

Slice was launching a card for millennials and Gen Z. I made the case that the product experience should begin at the letterbox.

Slice was launching a card for millennials and Gen Z. The category was commoditized and every card came in a standard envelope, every card looked more or less like every other card, and the product experience was assumed to begin after activation. I made the case that it should begin at the letterbox.

Decisions that mattered

Packaging as product instead of logistics. The premise most fintechs work from is that the card is the product and the envelope is the container. I had the opposite argument: the envelope is the first two minutes of the customer's relationship with the brand, and two minutes is a long time in fintech. The reference wasn't another bank; it was Apple. Minimal, tactile, high-quality, the kind of package a user films themselves opening. That framing changed the cost conversation from "why are we spending more on packaging" to "why aren't we spending more on the first product moment."

A 500-user test to de-risk the ask. Leadership wasn't going to sign off on premium packaging for the whole customer base on the strength of an aesthetic argument. So I proposed a bounded test with 500 users for a measured response and revert to standard packaging if it didn't work. This is the move that got it green-lit. Senior design work is usually 30% craft and 70% getting the room to say yes.

Card design in service of the unboxing, not the other way around. The card itself was designed to be the payoff of the sequence. It had to be clean, minimal, visually quiet, designed to look good in a fifteen-second phone video. Most card designs try to be memorable in isolation. This one was designed to be memorable in context.

What I'd do differently

I'd have proposed a second cohort. Same packaging but deliberately with a slightly inferior card design so I could isolate which part of the sequence was doing the work. The test we ran proved the whole experience landed. It didn't prove which decision inside the experience earned the response. Knowing that would have made the next three packaging decisions faster.

Results

  • Users organically posted unboxing videos to YouTube and the packaging did marketing the marketing team didn't have to fund.
  • Packaging became a permanent brand and growth lever at Slice, not a one-off launch flourish.
  • The card design is still in market years later.
