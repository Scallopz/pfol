Designing an interface for war

Designed the SURGE operator interface from scratch — little published precedent, validated with soldiers at expos because traditional user testing isn’t available when your users are on active duty.

At Armory, we build counter-drone systems. Our first product, SURGE, runs on Samaritan OS. The backend that handles compute and the frontend an operator uses to detect a drone, point an antenna at it, and jam it. I designed the operator interface from scratch. There is no meaningful precedent for this in Indian defence, and very little published reference internationally. Everything had to be reasoned from first principles and validated with soldiers at expos, because traditional user testing isn't available when your users are on active duty.

Decisions that mattered

1. Showing altitude on a 2D screen.

A drone's height is critical — you can't jam what you can't point at — but a tablet is flat. Every reference I could find either abstracted altitude into a number or used a second view, both of which are too slow for a war-time operator. I took the solution from FPS games. Call of Duty and Counter-Strike show elevation on their minimaps through position and marker treatment, and operators intuit it instantly because the pattern is familiar. On the SURGE radar I designed a set of concentric circles with the operator at the center and I let the operator tilt the tablet to move the radar circle up or down using the built-in gyroscope. Tilt up, the antenna points up. It removes a decision layer and turns altitude into a physical gesture instead of a menu.

2. Offset over calibration.

GPS drifts. In peacetime, you have the convenience to calibrate but in a war-time scenario, you don't have three minutes to calibrate a compass while a hostile drone is inbound. I added a manual offset so that the operator sees the discrepancy, dials in the correction, and moves on. It's a small feature but it came directly from watching how soldiers actually behaved at demos: they wanted to fix the symptom and not the system.

3. Three modes for one job.

Map, radar, and jammer. Each mode is optimised for a different decision: situational awareness, threat prioritisation, engagement, and the operator switches by task rather than by preference. Same underlying data but with three different views. Light and dark mode are separate from this and driven by operational style but not aesthetics: dark for night operations and covered positions, light for daylight and inside vehicles.

4. Friendly-drone tagging.

Operators can pre-register their own drones by signal ID so the system doesn't flag them as threats during an engagement. It sounds obvious in hindsight but it wasn't in the workflow when I inherited the problem.

5. In-app tutorials.

A new soldier can learn the system inside the app without a manual or a trainer. First of its kind in Indian defence, as far as I've been able to confirm. Multilingual too, also first of its kind. Both came from the same observation at demos: the operator using the system isn't always the one who was trained on it.

What I'd do differently

Prototyping the UI in Claude Design let engineers work in parallel from day one instead of waiting on hand-built components. That worked. What I'd change is how early I involved soldiers in the interaction model. I validated the visual language early but held the interaction decisions closer than I needed to. The gyroscope-elevation idea would have landed a full sprint sooner if I'd tested it at the first expo instead of the third.

Results

  • Live in hundreds of military units.
  • Contributed to Armory's ₹100Cr+ Ministry of Defence order.
  • First Indian counter-drone system with in-app tutorials and multilingual support.
